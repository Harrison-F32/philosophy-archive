On Denoting

Bertrand Russell
The Central Problem

How can we meaningfully talk about things that may not exist?

Russell begins with a problem about phrases that appear to refer to objects. Consider expressions such as “the present King of France,” “the author of Waverley,” or “a man.” They seem to stand for things, but Russell argues that their logical structure is more complicated than ordinary grammar suggests.

The central problem is what happens when a description appears to refer to something that does not exist. If there is no present King of France, what are we talking about when we say “the present King of France is bald”?

Russell's answer is his theory of descriptions. He argues that descriptions should not generally be treated as names that directly refer to objects.

Key Terms

Denoting Phrase

An expression that allows us to speak about an object or objects through a description rather than simply giving a name.

Definite Description

A phrase of the form “the so-and-so” that appears to identify one particular object.

Logical Form

The underlying logical structure of a proposition, which may differ from the grammatical structure of the sentence.

Theory of Descriptions

Russell's account of how definite descriptions can be analyzed without treating them as ordinary names.

The Theory of Descriptions

What does “the” actually mean?

Russell's most important claim is that a phrase such as “the present King of France” does not function like a simple proper name. Instead, a sentence containing the description has a more complex logical structure.

When we say “the present King of France is bald,” we are not referring to some nonexistent King of France and then saying something about him. The sentence can instead be analyzed into several separate claims.

Existence

There is at least one individual who satisfies the description.

Uniqueness

There is no more than one individual who satisfies the description.

Predication

The individual satisfying the description possesses the property being asserted.

The Present King of France

A famous problem in reference

Russell's most famous example is the sentence:

“The present King of France is bald.”

There is no present King of France. Nevertheless, the sentence appears to be perfectly meaningful. Russell argues that we do not need to assume that a nonexistent King of France exists in order to understand the sentence.

Instead, the sentence contains a claim of existence, a claim of uniqueness, and a claim about the resulting individual. Because there is no present King of France, the existence condition fails. The proposition is therefore false rather than meaningless.

Why Descriptions Are Not Names

Grammar can hide logical structure

Ordinary language makes “the present King of France” look like the grammatical subject of the sentence. Russell argues that this appearance is misleading.

A proper name such as “Socrates” and a description such as “the teacher of Plato” may appear to perform similar grammatical functions, but they do not necessarily have the same logical form.

Philosophical analysis must therefore distinguish between the way a sentence appears grammatically and what it actually asserts logically.

The grammatical form of a sentence does not always reveal its logical form.
Descriptions and Existence

We do not need nonexistent objects

One of Russell's major motivations is to avoid treating nonexistent objects as though they were real entities.

If every meaningful description had to name an object, then phrases such as “the present King of France” would seem to require some nonexistent King as their reference. Russell's theory avoids this problem by analyzing the description itself.

The sentence can be meaningful even though no object satisfies the description.

Russell's Larger Project

Logical analysis and philosophical problems

Russell's theory of descriptions is part of a larger project: using logical analysis to uncover the structure hidden beneath ordinary language.

He believes that many philosophical problems arise because the grammar of ordinary language encourages us to assume that words and phrases function in ways that they do not actually function logically.

By analyzing descriptions, Russell attempts to show how apparently mysterious statements can be understood without introducing unnecessary objects or entities.

Frege's distinction between sense and reference provides an important background to Russell's concerns about meaning, reference, and identity.

Bertrand Russell

Russell moves the discussion toward logical analysis, arguing that definite descriptions can be analyzed rather than treated as names directly standing for objects.

Connections

On Sense and Reference

Frege's essay examines how expressions can have different senses while sharing the same reference. Russell's theory approaches related problems about meaning and reference from a different logical perspective.

On Referring

Strawson later challenges Russell's account by arguing that reference depends heavily on how expressions are actually used in particular contexts.
