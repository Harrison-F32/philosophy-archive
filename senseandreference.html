On Sense and Reference

Gottlob Frege
The Central Problem

How can two names refer to the same thing but mean something different?

Frege begins with a problem about identity. Consider the statements “the Morning Star is the Morning Star” and “the Morning Star is the Evening Star.” The first appears trivial, while the second can represent a genuine discovery.

Yet both “Morning Star” and “Evening Star” refer to the same object: Venus. Frege's distinction between sense and reference explains how this is possible.

Two Aspects of Meaning

Reference

The object to which an expression refers. The references of “Morning Star” and “Evening Star” are both Venus.

Sense

The particular mode of presentation through which an object is given or thought of. The two expressions present Venus differently.

The Morning Star and Evening Star

One reference, two senses

The Morning Star is the bright object observed in the morning sky. The Evening Star is the bright object observed in the evening sky. Although these descriptions pick out the same astronomical object, they present that object in different ways.

Reference: Venus
Sense of “Morning Star”: Venus as presented in the morning
Sense of “Evening Star”: Venus as presented in the evening

Therefore, learning that the Morning Star and Evening Star are identical can give us new knowledge. The reference is the same, but the sense is different.

Identity

A = A versus A = B

Frege's puzzle becomes clearer when we compare two identity statements. “A = A” appears to tell us nothing new. “A = B,” however, can tell us something we did not previously know.

If the meaning of a name were nothing more than its reference, there would be no obvious reason for this difference. Frege therefore argues that expressions must also have a sense: a way in which their reference is presented.

The same object can be presented in different ways.
Why It Matters

From language to thought

Frege's distinction is not merely about different words for the same object. It explains how language can contain information and how two people can understand an expression while approaching the same object through different modes of presentation.

The distinction also becomes important when Frege discusses sentences, thoughts, and contexts in which expressions do not behave according to their ordinary reference.

Connections

Bertrand Russell

Russell's theory of descriptions takes up related problems about reference, descriptions, and how language connects with objects.

P. F. Strawson

Strawson later challenges aspects of Russell's account of referring, shifting attention toward how expressions are actually used in ordinary language.

Key Terms

Sense

The mode of presentation associated with an expression.

Reference

The object or entity to which an expression refers.
