On Sense and Reference

Gottlob Frege

The Central Problem

Frege begins with a puzzle about identity. If two expressions refer to the same object, how can a statement identifying them provide genuine information?

For example, the expressions "the Morning Star" and "the Evening Star" both refer to Venus. Yet discovering that the Morning Star and the Evening Star are the same object is informative. Frege's distinction between sense and reference explains why.

Sense and Reference

Reference

The object or entity that an expression actually picks out.

Sense

The particular way in which that object is presented or thought of through the expression.

Reference

What the expression stands for or denotes.

Sense

The mode of presentation through which the reference is given to us.

The Morning Star and Evening Star

Both "Morning Star" and "Evening Star" refer to Venus. Their references are therefore identical.

Their senses, however, differ. One presents Venus as the bright object appearing in the morning sky, while the other presents it as the bright object appearing in the evening.

This explains why the statement "The Morning Star is the Evening Star" can be informative. The two expressions have the same reference but different senses.

Frege's Puzzle About Identity

Frege distinguishes between two kinds of identity statements. "A = A" appears trivial, while "A = B" can be a genuine discovery.

If A and B had nothing more than their reference, both statements would seem to contain exactly the same information. The distinction between sense and reference explains the difference.

The same object can be presented through different senses.

Why Sense Matters

Frege's distinction is ultimately a theory about language and thought. A person can understand two expressions as conveying different information even when both expressions refer to the same object.

This becomes especially important when Frege considers sentences, thoughts, and contexts in which expressions no longer have their ordinary reference.

Connections

Frege's distinction became enormously influential in the philosophy of language and directly connects to later debates about reference and meaning.

Russell later challenged important aspects of Frege's account through his theory of descriptions, particularly in On Denoting.

Strawson subsequently criticized Russell's treatment of referring expressions in On Referring, bringing the discussion toward ordinary language.
